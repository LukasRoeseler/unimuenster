Why this matters

This is a non-normal distribution: heavily right-skewed, with most articles receiving relatively few citations and a small number receiving an enormous number. It looks nothing like the bell-shaped "normal" distribution that a lot of statistical intuition assumes, and that shape makes it very difficult to predict how many citations any individual article published in this journal will actually receive.

The solid mean line and the dashed median line above show why: in the full dataset the mean sits several times higher than the median, because it is dragged upward by a handful of extremely highly cited articles. A single outlier (try the field on the left) can shift the mean far more than it shifts the median, which is exactly why the mean is such an unstable summary of a skewed distribution.

The dotted h-index line is a third way to summarize the same sample: the largest number h for which h works each have at least h citations. Popularized as a researcher-level metric, it is sometimes applied to journals too, because like the median it resists being dragged around by one or two extreme outliers, unlike the mean, though it still grows with sample size in a way the median doesn't.

This is the underlying reason citation-based journal metrics — the Journal Impact Factor, OpenAlex's mean citedness, and similar — are contested: they report a mean of a distribution where the mean is not a stable or representative number. The median is more robust, but even it can't tell you how an individual article, including yours, will actually perform. The San Francisco Declaration on Research Assessment (DORA) recommends against using journal-level citation metrics to judge individual articles or researchers for exactly this reason.
